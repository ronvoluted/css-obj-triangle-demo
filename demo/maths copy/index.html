The maths, explained for humans

Four ideas turn a flat <div> into a face of a 3D cube. Each one below is a knob you can turn. No formulas you need to memorise — just intuition you can feel.

1. A div can't be a triangle — so we paint one

HTML only gives us rectangles. To get a triangle we keep the square box and colour exactly half of it with a hard-edged diagonal gradient. The colour stops and transparency starts at the same 50% line, so the seam becomes the triangle's long edge. Slide the split, and toggle the box to see the rectangle that's really there.

50%

Why a gradient and not clip-path? Inside a 3D context browsers don't reliably clip transformed children — but a gradient is painted into the box before the 3D transform, so it always survives.

2. The matrix is just "where do the corners go?"

matrix3d() looks scary — sixteen numbers — but for a triangle it answers three simple questions: where does corner A sit, and which way do its two edges point. Drag corner B and corner C below (A stays pinned at the centre) and watch the matrix columns change. Each column is one of those answers.

columns 1–2 — the two edges leaving corner A (B and C directions)
column 3 — the normal, computed for free as edge1 × edge2 (which way the face points)
column 4 — the position of corner A

3. Perspective is the camera lens

A rotation on its own just squashes things. Perspective is the rule "things farther away look smaller." It lives on the parent, and the number is how far your eye is from the screen — small numbers mean a wide, dramatic lens; big numbers flatten it out. Turn perspective off entirely and the 3D illusion collapses.

Same triangle, same rotation in all cases. The only thing changing is the lens on the parent — yet it's the difference between a flat shape and a plane standing in space.

4. The normal tells CSS which faces to hide

Every triangle has a normal: an arrow pointing out of its front. CSS compares that arrow to the camera. With backface-visibility: hidden, any triangle whose arrow points away gets skipped — so on a solid object you never see the inside of the far faces. Spin this cube and toggle the setting to watch the back faces blink out.

With culling on, the cube looks solid and clean. Turn it off and you'll catch translucent back faces showing through — the normal is what makes the difference, and we got it for free from the cross product in step 2.
